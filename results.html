Module 1 – Action recognition

Five actions – falling, standing still ("idle"), lifting an object, sitting down, walking – learned from 50 clips of 5 seconds each (10 per action). Evaluated by 5-fold cross-validation, on clips unseen in training. The final model (an LSTM with the velocity of the points computed inside the network, a ~1.9 s time-sampled window) reaches 88.5% window accuracy, 94.0% clip accuracy, a macro F1 of 0.88 and an MCC of 0.86.

The "idle" class (standing still, including with the hands held out, as when holding an object) also improved the other classes, because the model no longer has to fit stillness into an action; it has an F1 of 0.93 itself. On the live path (smoothing and the 60% threshold), on unseen clips, standing still is shown as "idle" 96% of the time and "uncertain" only 2%; 47 of the 50 clips are recognized correctly.

Modules 1 + 2 together – museum objects in your hands

In front of the webcam, whoever holds out their hands gets a museum object in them, at its real size, harmonized with the camera image, in real time and in Full HD (~30 frames per second on an RTX 2060, as many as the camera gives), while the action classifier of Module 1 runs in parallel. Every person gets their own objects:

  • one hand – a small object (up to 25 cm) on the palm, or a weapon held by its grip (a dagger, a spear, a war hammer); the N and H keys change the object and switch the harmonization on and off;
  • both hands close together (under 50 cm) – one large object, held at its belly with open palms or by its handles with the fingers closed, or a sword lying across the palms;
  • both hands far apart (over 70 cm) – one small object in each hand;
  • with both hands up, the objects change by themselves every 4 s, in shuffled order, as if dealt from a deck of cards: every object appears once before any comes again (then the deck is shuffled again), never the same one in both hands and never passed from one hand to the other at the next change, and each one is shown 2 s harmonized and 2 s raw, so that the difference can be seen without any key.

The hands are found with MediaPipe Hand Landmarker (21 points per hand, with 3D coordinates in metres, which give the real scale) and tied to the person they belong to through the MediaPipe Pose skeleton. The objects are cut out once with YOLOv8m + SAM 2.1 + matting; the harmonization runs in a separate process, on the GPU, as a 3×4 colour matrix fitted to the ensemble of networks, plus the camera's grain; the object keeps the sharpness of its photo.

The cut-out also works for objects YOLO does not know (helmets, the breastplate, the bust, the weapons): SAM starts from the largest weak detection in the photo, and where YOLO sees nothing or only a part (the celestial globe: the sphere, without Pegasus and the base; the two-handed sword, the horse-head defences, the armour), the catalog gives the box of the whole object. Small holes that SAM leaves inside an object are filled before the matting, and the soft edge of a cut-out is at most as wide as that of a 1600 px photo: on the 4000 px photos of the swords, the blades would otherwise come out partly see-through. The museum photographs its weapons upright. The swords are turned to lie flat in their cut-outs (the photos stay unchanged), have their real length instead of a height in the catalog and lie across both palms at their full length; the dagger, the spears and the war hammer are held in one hand, standing on the open palm by their lower end or gripped by the handle in a fist. Objects without handles are held in a grip by their widest upper part (the rim of a helmet or of a shield, the ring of the globe), and the bust and the swords as by open hands.

The camera is read at its maximum resolution, Full HD (1920×1080). The hands and the skeletons are found in a 960×540 copy of the frame (the models work on small images anyway), and the objects are drawn into the Full HD image, from cut-outs at the full resolution of the museum's photos (mostly 900–2300 px on the long side; the swords, lying flat, ~3600 px long, from the museum's 4000 px originals), so the incised lines, the engravings and the patterns of the paintings are clearly visible. The grain of the objects is the camera's noise, measured on the Full HD image itself: cameras smooth and compress the image, so their noise does not grow when it is enlarged (the camera used: σ ≈ 0.25 grey levels at 1080p, 0.42 on a 480-row copy).

Real-time drawing. A large vase held close to the camera reaches 1500–1800 px in height, more than the frame. The previous version resized the cut-out in floating point for every new size, and the hands always move, so the size changed almost every frame: drawing such a vase took 62 ms, and the image dropped to 8–10 frames per second. Now the cut-out is prepared once, with its colours extended under the transparent area (pull-push), so that resizing does not bring dark edges in. A vase much smaller than its photo is resized whole with INTER_AREA, as before, and kept while its size and the harmonization do not change; one close to the size of its photo or larger (over ~70%, a large vase held close) is sampled bilinearly every frame, only where it is seen (at these ratios, practically the same as INTER_AREA). The colour is applied once per harmonization update, on the cut-out, and the compositing is done directly in the 8-bit image (blendLinear), in reused arrays. The vases look the same as before (a mean difference of 0.1–0.5 grey levels, a median PSNR of 48–52 dB per vase) and no longer have the dark ring on their edges.

The scene's light, the vases' real colours

The harmonization networks are trained to repair objects whose colours are wrong, so they pull the object's colours towards the scene's. The museum photos, however, have the right colours; compared with the camera's scene, only the light differs. Applied in full, the networks visibly changed the colours of the vases: they washed them out in a sunny square and turned them yellow in a warm hall. That is why, live, the change of brightness and contrast is taken from the networks in full, but only 25% of the change of colour. The measurements are made with exactly the live code (live_harmony_eval.py), on the harmonization benchmark (30 cases from real photos) and on the 8 vases put into 5 real scenes.

Brightness and contrast alone bring 81% of the harmonization's gain on the benchmark (from 21.3 to 25.7 dB, out of 26.8 dB). With 25% of the colour change, the vases practically keep the colours of their museum photos, and in a warm light they still get a warm tint. On the benchmark only 0.6 dB is lost, because there the objects have wrong colours by construction. The colour matrix used live is even slightly better than the networks applied pixel by pixel (26.8 dB vs 26.5 dB): as a global transform, it smooths out their local artefacts.

The painting and inscriptions of harmonized vases

Once harmonized, a vase used to lose almost all its detail: the painting, the incised lines, the inscriptions. The "camera" match blurred it down to the sharpness measured on the hands holding it, but the skin of the hands is smooth and always reads as blurred, so the blur sat at its cap (1.5 px at 480 rows, i.e. 3.4 px in Full HD) in 55% of the updates, with the median right at the cap. The camera, however, is not blurry: measured on the sharpest edges of its own frames, its transitions take 1.25 px (10–90%), less than those of a vase drawn without any blur (1.75 px). So a vase held in the hands, at the same distance as they are, is no longer blurred: the harmonization changes only its light and tone (the colour matrix, with 25% of the colour change) and adds the camera's grain. In addition, the cut-outs now keep the full resolution of the photos (973–1314 px, not cropped to 1080 px), and the two large amphorae, which the hands bring closest to the camera (up to ~1800 px on screen), are made from the museum's originals downscaled to 2400 px (2031 and 2099 px): on screen they are downscaled, not enlarged.

In the C++ app

The Qt/C++ app shows the objects exactly like the Python demo, with the same rules, automatic rhythm, harmonization and keys (H, O, N, D, S, Q), next to the action recognition, also in Full HD. Python (pose_server.py) does the perception and the decisions, with the same code: the hands, the people, which object appears, where and how it is held, and the harmonization. For every frame, it sends the description of the objects (cut-out, size, position, way of holding, the 3×4 colour matrix, the sharpness and the noise). C++ (object_renderer.cpp) composes the image step by step as in Python, on the very frame in which the hands were found, so that the objects do not lag behind them.

The test scene contains all three ways of holding (on the palm, with a shadow; at the belly and by the handles, with the hands in front of the object), partial opacity and an object cut by the edge of the frame; the differences of 1–2 levels (4 pixels out of 48,000) come from rounding. The sensor noise added by C++ has the requested deviation (3.98 measured, for 4). Drawing the three objects takes ~0.9 ms at 640×480 and ~4 ms in Full HD (Release); with the camera, the app shows ~27–30 frames per second in Full HD, also with the objects in hand (the camera gives 30; fewer after many minutes of running, when the laptop's processor heats up). The Python server has two frames at a time: while it finds the hands in one, the next one is already read and decoded, and C++ prepares the frame after it while it draws the answer.

Along with the integration, tying the hands to people became robust to the moments when the skeleton model loses a person. A person lost for a moment keeps their identity, and a hand whose body is missing in a frame stays with the person it belonged to. Composition: the previous version vs the new one

For the moved object to look as if it belongs in the picture, the new pipeline deals with every cue by which the eye recognizes a composite:

  1. Precise cut-out – SAM 2.1 starts from the box YOLO finds and follows the exact shape of the object (handles, contour), leaving the base out.
  2. Natural edges – matting with a guided filter, and the edge colours cleaned of the old background (no "halo").
  3. The right size and perspective – Depth Anything V2 gives the distance in metres; the object's real height gives its size in the image, and the floor plane gives the perspective of the shadow.
  4. Detail – Real-ESRGAN restores the detail when the object has to be enlarged.
  5. Colour and light – the mean of three networks trained on iHarmony4 (Harmonizer, PCT-Net ViT, PCT-Net CNN).
  6. Sharpness and noise brought to the level of the scene.
  7. A reflection in the glossy floor and a contact shadow in the floor's perspective.

In each pair, the top image is the previous version (YOLO mask, blurred edge, Harmonizer, no super-resolution, reflection or shadow), at the same size and position; the bottom one is the new version. The last panel enlarges the area of the object.

Scale and perspective from depth

Depth Anything V2 (its metric version) estimates the distance in metres of every background pixel. Knowing the object's real height H and the distance Z at the point where it is placed, its height in the image is h = f · H / Z (a pinhole camera, with an assumed 60° field of view). A plane fitted to the 3D points of the surrounding floor tells how "flattened" the floor appears (its tilt), used for the ellipse of the contact shadow. The first panel shows the depth map (near = bright), with the contact point marked.

Harmonization and relighting: 11 methods

Colour harmonization does not change the direction of the light on the object. Two recent methods that relight the object were also tested, both running on an RTX 2060 (12–20 s per image, peak memory 4.7 GB and 4.4 GB):

  • Intrinsic Harmonization (Careaga et al., SIGGRAPH Asia 2023): decomposes the image into albedo and shading, harmonizes the albedo of the object and re-shades it under the light estimated from the background (spherical harmonics from the scene's shading and normals).
  • Ensemble + re-shading (the proposed combination): the colours of the ensemble of three networks, then only the re-shading step of the method above.
  • IC-Light (Zhang et al., ICLR 2025): a diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 1.5) that regenerates the object lit as in the background; only the change of light is kept, as a smooth gain map, so that the details (the painting of the vase) are not altered.

The figures are cropped around the object; the full images are in data/harmonization_results/.

Conclusion: on an RTX 2060 both relighting methods run, but neither of them is safe for every object. Intrinsic Harmonization over-corrects the albedo of coloured objects (the orange of terracotta turns olive or greenish); the "ensemble + re-shading" variant keeps the colours and changes only the shading. IC-Light gives the most convincing result on the person (the warm tone of the skin matches the hall), but it saturates the terracotta vases. The default method remains the ensemble, the most accurate on the benchmark.

The ensemble of the three networks remains the best method on every metric (fMSE 191, fPSNR 26.5 dB, fSSIM 0.949): it reduces the error on the object by 32% compared with Harmonizer (the previous version of the pipeline) and by 69% compared with the unharmonized image. The classical methods make the result worse, because they assume the object should have the colours of the background.

The relighting methods score lower on this benchmark, as expected: here the object is only recoloured in its own photo, so its lighting is already right, and any change of shading is penalized. The benchmark measures colour correction, not lighting. For real composites, where the object comes from another light (studio → museum hall), relighting can help visually (for example IC-Light on the person), but it can also exaggerate (the Cypriot amphora becomes too orange). That is why the pipeline keeps the ensemble by default, and relighting remains optional (--harmony intrinsic | reshade | iclight).
